      How it works

      The same pipeline as the Python notebook, ported to JavaScript. On every one of the 5,169 messages, the browser version gives the same probability as scikit-learn.

      1. Normalise

        Lowercase the text and swap phone numbers, prices, links and e-mails for placeholders like [PHONE] and [MONEY]. The model learns that a message has a premium number without memorising which one.

      2. Vectorise

        TF-IDF over words and word pairs (12,074 features) plus 3–5 character fragments (23,674 features). Fragments catch spellings like txt, ur and fr33.

      3. Score

        Class-weighted logistic regression adds up each feature's weight. The total becomes a spam probability, and anything at 50% or above is flagged.

      4. Explain

        Each feature's share of the score is assigned back to the word it came from. That share sets the colour of each word in the verdict panel.

      Results on held-out messages

      The models were trained on 80% of the data and tested on the other 1,034 messages, which they never saw. Only 12.6% of messages are spam, so the table reports spam precision, recall and F1 alongside accuracy.

      Why logistic regression here

      The linear SVM scores slightly higher in the notebook (F1 0.958 vs 0.954), but logistic regression outputs a real probability. That gives the gauge an honest scale.

      Why accuracy misleads

      A model that calls every message ham is 87% accurate and catches no spam at all. Spam F1 is the number to compare.

      Limits

      The training data is UK SMS from around 2012. Modern scams like parcel-delivery texts, OTP fraud and crypto offers use different wording, so expect misses on those.
